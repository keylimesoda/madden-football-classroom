UNIT 00
Field Vision I

Offensive formation: know your people before you read theirs

Canonical teaching baseline: true shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2 (RB beside QB; X + slot left; attached TE + Z right) versus standard nickel 4-2-5 (two ends, two tackles, Mike, Will, nickel, two corners, two safeties).

On the board: Keep your eyes on the field landmarks while you work through the look. The alignment tells you where the next decision starts.

Time: about fifteen minutes

Football is like chess. You can win a game without a brilliant move if you consistently know where the pieces are, what each piece can do, and which piece has been left exposed.

That starts before the snap. Before you decide whether the defense is one-high or two-high, before you count the box, before you even think about a hot route, look at the eleven players wearing your jersey. Who is on the field? Where are they lined up? What can this formation become after the ball moves?

A defense sees all of that too. Personnel and formation are the first messages an offense sends across the line of scrimmage. Good offenses learn to send one message and play a different song.

Personnel: who is on the field?

Offensive personnel is written with two numbers. The first number is running backs; the second is tight ends. The rest of the skill players are wide receivers.

So **11 personnel** means one running back and one tight end. That leaves three wide receivers.

12 personnel means one running back and two tight ends. That leaves two wide receivers.

13 personnel means one running back and three tight ends. That leaves one wide receiver.

21 personnel means two running backs and one tight end. That leaves two wide receivers.

22 personnel means two running backs and two tight ends. That leaves one wide receiver.

The math always adds up to five eligible receivers. The offensive line is still five players. The quarterback is still one player. Personnel tells you how the five eligible receivers are built; alignment tells you where they go.

Do not hear “12 personnel” and assume run. Do not hear “11 personnel” and assume pass. Personnel gives the defense a starting expectation. It does not hand them the answer.

Eleven personnel can spread the field with five players eligible in the route. Thirteen personnel can show a heavy surface, then release tight ends into a route combination that strains coverage. A back can motion out of the backfield. A tight end can shift from attached to the slot. The same people can make the defense solve a different geometry after it has already chosen a call.

That is the first offensive advantage: make the defense declare how it wants to handle your people, then make it live with the answer.

The line is the spine of the formation

Start at the center. He has a guard on each side. Outside the guards sit the tackles. That five-man line is the spine of the offense.

Traditionally, the left side gets called the “pretty side” and the right side the “ugly side.” The old shorthand comes from blind-side protection: a right-handed quarterback usually sees pressure from his right, while the left tackle handles the defender coming from the quarterback’s blind side. Teams have often valued different body types and skill sets at each tackle spot—more finesse in space on the left, more power on the right—but do not turn that into a law. Modern offenses ask both tackles to pass protect, reach, pull, down-block, and handle movement. The individual player and the concept matter more than the nickname.

Everything else lines up around that spine.

An attached tight end lines up next to a tackle. The side with the tight end is usually the **strong side**. The other side is the **weak side**. That label is not decoration. The tight end can become an extra edge blocker, a receiver in the seam or flat, a motion player, or the player that forces the defense to add a bigger body to one side of the formation.

When you hear “run it to strength,” you are usually hearing “run it toward the tight end side.” That does not mean the ball must go there. Weak-side runs, split flow, counters, and backside cuts exist precisely because defenses lean toward the strength they can see.

Start with the quarterback: under center or in gun?

Next, find the quarterback.

Under center means the quarterback is directly behind the center. The exchange happens immediately. That helps the offense get downhill quickly on handoffs and sells play-action from a compact backfield. The tradeoff is that a passing quarterback has to gain depth before he can settle into a conventional drop.

Shotgun puts the quarterback several yards behind the center. The snap travels farther, but the quarterback starts with a better view of the front and does not need to take as many steps just to get into throwing depth. In Madden, shotgun also gives you a clearer pre-snap look at the box, safeties, and leverage—but the defense knows you are looking at the same picture.

The running back tells you more about the shape of the offense.

Under center, the halfback is often directly behind the quarterback. In I formation, a fullback may sit between the quarterback and the halfback. That fullback is not there to carry the ball very often. He is there to find a linebacker, kick out an edge player, lead through the hole, or sell a downhill run before the offense breaks into play-action.

In shotgun, the back usually aligns beside the quarterback. That offset can matter. The back’s side affects pass protection, run direction, motion, RPO presentation, and which side of the quarterback has a quick checkdown.

Most modern NFL and Madden rosters do not carry a traditional fullback. When a Madden formation calls for one, the game may use a backup tight end, a power back, or another available player in that role. The label matters less than the job: somebody is being asked to lead, block, release, or threaten the defense from the backfield.

Receiver distribution: how is the field divided?

Once you know the personnel and the backfield, count how the eligible receivers are distributed from left to right.

The cleanest receiver-distribution language counts the wide/slot receivers on each side:

  • **2×2:** two receivers left, two receivers right. The fifth eligible may be an attached tight end, a back in the backfield, or another receiver depending on the formation.
  • **3×1:** three receivers to one side—often called **trips**—and one receiver to the other. The fifth eligible is again accounted for by the tight end or back.
  • **Bunch:** three receivers clustered tightly enough to create natural traffic and release problems.
  • **Stack:** two receivers aligned one behind the other, often to protect a release or force the defense to communicate.
  • **Empty:** the backfield is empty. The running back has split out or is otherwise not beside/behind the quarterback.
Do not confuse receiver distribution with personnel. A team can be in 11 personnel and line up 3×1. It can be in 11 and spread into empty. It can be in 13 and still create a serious pass picture if the tight ends detach, shift, or release into routes.

Distribution changes the defense’s math. Trips can force the nickel, linebacker, safety, and corner to sort out three threats on one side. A 2×2 set can stretch the defense more evenly and reveal how it wants to handle each side. Bunch and stack make man coverage communicate through traffic. Empty tells the defense the back is no longer in the box—but it also strips a potential pass protector from the quarterback.

Formation is not a play call. Formation is the starting problem you hand the defense.

Put it together: the baseline look

For the rest of MFC 101, the home formation is **shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2, TE right**:

  • quarterback in shotgun;
  • running back offset beside him;
  • attached tight end on the right;
  • X wide left and a slot receiver left;
  • attached TE right and Z wide right;
  • RB offset beside the quarterback as the fifth eligible player.
That is a true 2×2 distribution: X and slot left; attached TE and Z right. The RB remains beside the quarterback. It gives the offense two balanced receiving surfaces while keeping a real tight-end edge on the right.

Say it the way a quarterback would say it in the huddle:

“Gun 11. Two by two. TE right. Back offset.”

Now the defense has a real problem. Does it keep the nickel over the left slot or walk it toward the TE side? Does it lean an overhang toward the attached tight end? Does it shade a safety to either two-receiver surface? Those answers shape the next read.

Those are the questions that lead us into the defense. But first, know the people you brought to the line.

On the practice field

Open Practice Mode and choose three formations before you run a single play:

1. Shotgun 11, 2×2, with a tight end attached right and the RB offset beside the QB. 2. I formation or another under-center look with a lead blocker. 3. A 3×1 or trips formation from the same personnel family if the playbook offers it.

For each formation, say three things before you look at the defense:

“Personnel. QB location. Receiver distribution.”

Then make one prediction: *What kind of defender will the offense force the defense to put on the field or move toward the strength?*

Do not worry about being right on every prediction yet. The point is to stop seeing formations as menu names and start seeing them as problems you are giving the defense.

UNIT 01
Field Vision I

See the 4-2-5 nickel front

Canonical teaching baseline: true shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2 (RB beside QB; X + slot left; attached TE + Z right) versus standard nickel 4-2-5 (two ends, two tackles, Mike, Will, nickel, two corners, two safeties).

On the board: Keep your eyes on the field landmarks while you work through the look. The alignment tells you where the next decision starts.

Time: about ten minutes

Now that you can see your own formation, put a real defensive picture in front of it.

The base look here is a standard 4-2-5 nickel. Four down linemen. Two linebackers. Five defensive backs. It is a cleaner first picture because you can see the front and the second level without sorting through an odd-front disguise on day one.

Up front, find the two ends and two tackles. Behind them, find the Mike and Will. The nickel is the fifth DB, usually aligned to the three-receiver side. The front can still slant, stunt, or walk a linebacker up, but the starting picture is four down and two backers.

Behind the line, the Mike and Will can tighten, widen, walk toward the TE, lean toward trips, or become part of the pressure picture. Out wide, you have two corners and a nickel/star. Deep, two safeties begin the play.

Against your TE-right, three-receiver-right formation, keep an eye on the nickel. He may be over F. He may tighten toward the tight end. He may become an edge player, a flat player, or an extra body in the run picture. That player is not scenery.

What you should see

“Four down. Mike and Will behind them. Nickel to the trips side. Two corners. Two safeties.”

That sentence is a clean start. It does not tell you every assignment. It tells you where to keep watching.

The trap

A walked-up linebacker can make this front look heavier than it is. A safety creeping into the box can do the same. Start with the four true down linemen. Then ask which linebacker, nickel, or safety is close enough to change the play.

On the practice field

Put the defense in a 4-2-5 nickel look. Freeze before the snap. Circle the four down linemen. Then find Mike, Will, the nickel, corners, and safeties. If the nickel tightens toward the TE side, say what he can affect: the edge, the flat, or the run fit.

UNIT 02
Field Vision I

Follow the safeties through the snap

Canonical teaching baseline: true shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2 (RB beside QB; X + slot left; attached TE + Z right) versus standard nickel 4-2-5 (two ends, two tackles, Mike, Will, nickel, two corners, two safeties).

On the board: Keep your eyes on the field landmarks while you work through the look. The alignment tells you where the next decision starts.

Time: about fifteen minutes

This is where modern football gets honest.

The safeties tell you something before the snap. They do not tell you everything. If you see two high safeties and immediately shout “Cover 2,” a good defense has already won the rep. The whole point of disguise is to make you commit before the picture finishes developing.

Track the safeties in three moments.

Before the snap: what are they showing?

One deep middle safety is a one-high presentation. Two safeties split deep is a two-high presentation. Call that exactly what it is: a presentation.

Two high says: check the box, then see whether the split-safety structure survives. One high says: find the extra near-line defender and see whether the middle safety holds.

It does **not** say Cover 1, Cover 2, Cover 3, or quarters. Not yet.

At the snap: who moves first?

Watch the safeties through the quarterback’s first steps.

If one safety drives down and the other takes the deep middle, the defense has closed the middle. Your pre-snap split-safety picture is gone. That can become a Cover 3-like problem, but the useful first statement is simpler:

“The weak safety spun down. The middle closed.”

Now you know the original answer may need to change.

If both safeties stay high, keep watching. You still do not know whether the outside and underneath defenders will play the route conflicts like Cover 2, quarters, a match call, or something split-field.

Once routes threaten: who owns what?

Now the defense starts talking.

Watch the corner. Does he squat in the flat? Does he bail and carry vertical? Does he sink under an outside route? Watch the flat defender. Does he widen? Does he squeeze inside? Watch the safeties. Are they still splitting the field, or did one take over the middle?

That is the difference between staring at a shell and reading a coverage picture.

Two high can become middle closed

The defense shows two safeties. You think split field. The ball moves; the weak safety drives into the box; the strong safety becomes the deep middle player. You now have a middle-closed picture.

Do not keep throwing the answer you liked against two high just because it was there half a second ago. Update the picture.

Two high does not settle Cover 2 versus quarters

Both can start with two safeties high. The difference shows up when receivers threaten vertically and outside.

A Cover 2-like corner may be involved in the short outside/flat picture while the safety protects the deep half. A quarters/Cover 4-like defense can distribute vertical threats differently, with corners and safeties reading releases and matching threats. Madden calls and match rules can change the details. Your job is not to win a terminology contest from the first frame.

Your job is to say what happened:

“Two high before the snap. Both stayed high. The corner carried the outside vertical and the flat defender widened.”

That is a good football answer, even if you leave the final label unresolved.

On the practice field

Run a concept with an outside vertical, a flat route, and an inside route. On every rep, pause three times:

1. before the snap; 2. when the safeties declare their first move; 3. when the routes force the corners and flat defenders to show their work.

Write one line:

“They showed ____. Then ____. The corner/flat players ____. My first answer [stayed / changed] because ____.”

Do not name the coverage until the clip earns the name.

UNIT 03
Field Vision I

Count the box for the run you actually called

Canonical teaching baseline: true shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2 (RB beside QB; X + slot left; attached TE + Z right) versus standard nickel 4-2-5 (two ends, two tackles, Mike, Will, nickel, two corners, two safeties).

On the board: Keep your eyes on the field landmarks while you work through the look. The alignment tells you where the next decision starts.

Time: about twelve minutes

There is no magic box number.

Six can be light or loaded. Seven can be a good run picture or a bad one. The number only matters against the run you called, the tight end side, your blockers, and the defender who can insert late.

Start with the 4-2-5 front. The two tackles and two ends set the first run picture. Then find Mike and Will. Then look at the nickel and safeties. Are they hanging over receivers, or are they tight enough to change the edge or the interior fit?

Do not count every red jersey. Count the defenders who can hit **this run**.

Inside run

On an inside run, the nose, interior edges, Mike, and nearest second-level fit are the first conversation. If the nickel is wide over the slot, he may not be the immediate interior problem. If a safety rolls down late, now the count changed after the snap.

Say it like this:

“Four down, Mike and Will stacked. The nickel is wide. For inside zone, I am watching the Will fit and any late safety insert.”

Outside run toward the TE side

Now the tight end, strong overhang, nickel, and corner/force player matter differently. If the nickel squeezes tight to the TE/slot surface, he can change the edge even though he is a DB.

Say it like this:

“The nickel is tight to the TE side. He changes the edge count. I need to see whether we can reach him or whether the quick answer is cleaner.”

That is a football thought. “Seven means pass” is not.

On the practice field

Use one inside run and one outside run from the same formation. Run them against the same 4-2-5 front three ways: nickel wide, nickel tight, safety inserted.

Before the snap, tell yourself:

“For this run, the defender who changes the picture is ____.”

After the rep, find the first defender who actually changed the lane. If your answer was wrong, that is film work—not failure.

UNIT 04
Field Vision I

Read leverage, then find the help

Canonical teaching baseline: true shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2 (RB beside QB; X + slot left; attached TE + Z right) versus standard nickel 4-2-5 (two ends, two tackles, Mike, Will, nickel, two corners, two safeties).

On the board: Keep your eyes on the field landmarks while you work through the look. The alignment tells you where the next decision starts.

Time: about twelve minutes

The corner is always telling you something. He is rarely telling you the whole story.

Off coverage gives cushion. Press puts the release under stress. Inside leverage says the defender wants to protect inside access first. Outside leverage says he wants to protect the sideline first.

That is where you start looking. It is not where you automatically throw.

Off coverage

If the corner is six or seven yards off, you may have room for a quick completion. But look inside and over the top. Is the flat defender widening? Is a safety rolling into that window? Is the user defender sitting on the throw? Cushion gets you to the window; it does not throw the ball for you.

Press coverage

Press tells you the receiver has to win the release and the quarterback has to have time. A route can look beautiful on the play art and still arrive late because the receiver got knocked off schedule.

Inside and outside leverage

If a defender sits inside, peek outside first. If he protects the sideline, see whether the inside break is there. Then verify the help.

Against two-high, the safety may still own enough of the top to punish an impatient throw. Against a rotated middle-closed picture, the flat or hook defender may be waiting where the corner appeared to concede space.

A useful sentence

“The corner is off and inside. I will look outside first, but I need to see what the flat defender and safety do before I commit.”

That is much better than “off corner—throw it.”

On the practice field

Keep the same concept. Change only the corner or nickel alignment when possible. Log four things: the alignment, the first space you inspected, the help defender you watched, and what the defense actually did.

The goal is to make leverage a clue you can use under pressure—not a slogan you repeat after the game.

UNIT 05
Field Vision I

Build a plan that can survive the snap

Canonical teaching baseline: true shotgun 11 personnel, 2×2 (RB beside QB; X + slot left; attached TE + Z right) versus standard nickel 4-2-5 (two ends, two tackles, Mike, Will, nickel, two corners, two safeties).

On the board: Keep your eyes on the field landmarks while you work through the look. The alignment tells you where the next decision starts.

Time: about fifteen minutes

Now put it together.

You are not trying to solve football before the snap. You are trying to enter the snap with a plan that can survive the first change in the picture.

Start with your own formation. Then work out:

1. **Presentation:** What are the safeties showing? Where is the nickel? What does the front look like? 2. **Run picture:** Which defenders can hit the run you have available? 3. **Perimeter clue:** What is the corner or nickel giving you, and where is the help? 4. **First answer and safe answer:** What do you inspect first? What do you do if the safety spins, the flat widens, the corner squats, or pressure arrives?

That is the pre-snap scan. Shell → box → leverage → plan is the shorthand. The field work underneath it is what makes it useful.

Walk through the call

You are in shotgun 11, TE right. The defense shows a 4-2-5 nickel front. Two safeties are high. The nickel has tightened toward the TE/trips side. The outside corner to trips is off and outside.

Your thought process:

“Gun 11, TE right. Two high right now. Nickel is tight, so the outside run picture toward strength is crowded. The corner gives cushion, but I need to see the flat player and safety rotation. First, I check whether the safeties stay split and whether the flat widens. If they squeeze the quick answer or overfill the edge, I go to the inside safe answer. I am not forcing the pre-snap picture.”

That sounds like a quarterback, because it is a sequence of decisions—not a coverage guess.

When the picture changes

If the weak safety spins down and the middle closes, acknowledge it. If the corner squats and the flat defender widens, acknowledge it. Then take the answer that is still alive.

A good rep can end in an incompletion if the execution fails. A bad read can become a completion against a busted assignment. Do not grade your thinking from the stat line alone. Grade it from what the defense showed and what you chose to do with it.

On the practice field

Build a tiny menu from one formation: an inside run, an outside run, a quick perimeter concept, and an inside safe answer.

Take twelve reps:

  • four against one-high presentations;
  • four against two-high presentations;
  • four against looks that rotate after the snap.
Before each snap, say the whole plan out loud. After it, give the short film answer:
“What did they show? What changed? Did my first answer stay alive? Where was the safe answer?”

That is how the pre-snap scan becomes a habit instead of a paragraph.

Finish line

You are ready to move on when you can watch an unfamiliar look and speak a clean plan in twenty seconds:

“Formation. Shell. Run picture. Leverage. First answer. What changes it. Safe answer.”

If you can do that, you are not guessing at the defense anymore. You are making it answer you.
